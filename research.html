Research

Research areas and programs

Agency

How should we model artificial systems as agents with beliefs, preferences, and decision procedures?

Why it mattersMuch of AI safety depends on whether and when advanced systems can be meaningfully understood in agentic terms.

Evolutionary Theory of Advanced AI Adaptations

Led by Aydin Mohseni and Daniel Herrmann

A general theory of the pressures that shape the behavioral dispositions of advanced AI systems as they are trained and deployed, and of how those pressures bear on alignment. Current work gives an epistemic interpretation of the power-seeking theorems.

More

Which pressures shape the behavioral dispositions of AI systems as they are trained and deployed, and how do those pressures bear on alignment?

Claims that capable AI systems will seek power, resist shutdown, or deceive their overseers are usually stated loosely. We treat each such claim of instrumental convergenceInstrumental convergence: the thesis that agents with very different final goals will pursue similar intermediate goals, such as acquiring resources, because those goals help with almost any final goal. as a claim with six parts:

Systems of kind A, trained by processes T, on tasks and data distributed as D, converge in sense C to behavior pattern B in situations S.

The behavior patterns B in view include coherence, coordination, collusion, deception, learning, situational awareness, proxy gaming, power-seeking, resource acquisition, and self-preservation. Current work concerns power-seeking.

Current work: the power-seeking theorems

Turner and colleagues proved that, for most reward functions, optimal policies tend to seek power.A. M. Turner, L. Smith, R. Shah, A. Critch, and P. Tadepalli, “Optimal Policies Tend to Seek Power,” NeurIPS 2021; A. M. Turner and P. Tadepalli, “Parametrically Retargetable Decision-Makers Tend to Seek Power,” NeurIPS 2022. The proofs count reward functions by a symmetry argument that many readers find hard to interpret. We construct the prior that the counting assumes, which turns each result into a statement about probability. The probabilities then admit two interpretations.

Naturalizing Agency

Led by Daniel Herrmann

Claims about AI risk use the vocabulary of agency: a system seeks power, plans around its overseers, controls its environment. We show how such notions, starting with chance, causation, and control, can be recovered from an agent’s own beliefs and desires, so that claims made in this vocabulary become claims interpretability can test.

More

Can the notions we use to describe agents be recovered from the agent’s own beliefs and desires?

De Finetti showed that an agent who treats a sequence of trials as exchangeable behaves as if she were learning an unknown chance, so chance can be recovered from credence. Skyrms extended the program, treating chance as resilient credence.B. de Finetti, “La prévision: ses lois logiques, ses sources subjectives,” Annales de l’Institut Henri Poincaré 7 (1937); B. Skyrms, Causal Necessity, Yale University Press, 1980. We take the same approach to causation, counterfactuals, and an agent’s sense of what is under her control. Each looks like a fact about the world that an agent must track. The aim is to show that each can be recovered from her attitudes once she represents herself as part of the world she is trying to predict and change.

Current work: causation and control

Results on Pearl’s causal hierarchy seem to show that causal claims cannot be reduced to probabilistic ones.J. Pearl, Causality, Cambridge University Press, 2000; E. Bareinboim, J. D. Correa, D. Ibeling, and T. Icard, “On Pearl’s Hierarchy and the Foundations of Causal Inference,” 2022. The irreducibility comes from an impoverished set of variables. Add variables for interventions, treated as events in the world, and interventional claims become conditional probabilities:

P(Y = y | do(X = x)) = P(Y = y | X = x, IX = on).

For control, desirability tracking reads off, from an agent’s beliefs and desires, the propositions she takes herself to control and the decision problems she takes herself to face.

Why it matters for safety

Power-seeking is the pursuit of options an agent takes to be within its control. Planning around an overseer needs a world model that supports reasoning about interventions. Both claims presuppose an account of what a system treats as a cause and as within its power. If those can be read off its beliefs and desires, then the worry that a system seeks power or plans around its overseers becomes a claim about its attitudes, which the methods of our interpretability program can test. The adaptations program needs exactly this account of control.

Key questions

  1. When does a decision theory describe a trained system well enough to predict how its behavior generalizes?
  2. Can models that are causally isolated from one another still coordinate?
  3. What is a world model, and what does a causal model do for the agent that has one?

Interpretability

What evidence warrants attributing beliefs, goals, or representations to advanced AI systems?

Why it mattersDeployment decisions will rest on what probes and evaluations report. A probe that tracks a correlate of truth, and not a belief, fails silently, and a silent failure that feeds a deployment decision is worse than no measurement.

Measuring Beliefs and Desires in AI Systems

Led by Daniel Herrmann and Aydin Mohseni

When does an AI system have beliefs and goals, and how could we measure them? We develop criteria for attributing beliefs, desires, and meanings to AI systems, and test them on trained models, from small transformers to language models.

More

How can we tell what an AI system believes and wants from facts about its computation?

Interpretability methods read beliefs and goals off a model’s internal states. Whether a reading is correct depends on what it is for a state to be a belief, and the methods leave this unstated. We take up the problem of radical interpretation,Radical interpretation: the problem, posed by Davidson and Lewis, of working out what an agent believes, wants, and means from facts that do not presuppose any of these. with the facts about the agent given by its computation:

Find the beliefs B, desires D, and meanings M that together best explain the computation C of system S.

The three are solved for together. Belief, desire, and the structure of the propositions they concern constrain one another, so a method that holds one fixed while measuring another inherits its distortions.

Current work

Criteria. Probes for belief in language models fail to generalize in basic ways,B. A. Levinstein and D. A. Herrmann, “Still No Lie Detector for Language Models: Probing Empirical and Conceptual Roadblocks,” Philosophical Studies 182 (2025). and a representation must meet four conditions to count as belief-like.D. A. Herrmann and B. A. Levinstein, “Standards for Belief Representations in LLMs,” Minds and Machines 35 (2025). The book Radical AI Interpretability ties criteria of success, on representationalist and interpretationist views alike, to tests that current methods can run. The next step is to give deception and situational awareness the same treatment.

Model organisms. In a synthetic decision task, the true posterior over hidden states and the value of each option can be computed exactly, so attributions can be checked against ground truth. A small transformer trained on choices alone develops separable linear representations of both, and steering the posterior changes choice through the subspace that encodes the options’ values, as a Jeffrey–Bolker reading predicts. A pretrained language model shows the same organization. This work is led by Jeremy Saal, with Simplex; a paper is under review.

Language models. When a language model’s choices are read as expected-utility maximization, how coherent are the credences and utilities this attributes, and how robust is the attribution? We test whether it predicts choices in and out of sample, whether steering the putative credence and utility representations shifts choices as expected-utility theory predicts, whether it agrees with probes, and whether it is stable under meaning-preserving rephrasings.

Key questions

  1. What would make a probe for deception or situational awareness a measurement of it?
  2. What does an evaluation show when the system can tell that it is being evaluated?
  3. Probes are validated against behavior and then used to check behavioral evaluations. Where does that regress stop?

Value

How should we think about value uncertainty, preference, and normative learning?

Why it mattersTraining pipelines already settle whose feedback counts and how conflicts are resolved, usually without stating these as choices.

Decision under Value Uncertainty

Led by Aydin Mohseni and Daniel Herrmann

How should an agent choose when values are uncertain, whether her own values or those of someone she acts for? Both questions arise for AI systems meant to learn and serve what people value.

More

How should values that are not yet settled enter a decision?

Decision theory handles uncertainty about facts well and uncertainty about values badly. Two cases matter for AI: an agent refining her own values, and an agent acting for someone whose preferences she does not know.

Current work: learning what one values

Standard frameworks let an agent learn about the world while her options and values stay fixed. We extend the Jeffrey–Bolker framework so that values themselves can be refined, and prove a value-of-information theorem for this axiological refinement. Between agents, mutual refinement tends to turn zero-sum games into positive-sum ones, and can yield Pareto improvements in Nash bargaining.

Current work: acting for someone whose preferences are uncertain

A benefactor chooses on behalf of one person and is uncertain about that person’s preferences. The natural rule is to maximize expected utility across the candidate preferences. But each candidate utility function is fixed only up to a choice of scale, and rescaling one of them can change which option comes out best.Multiplying one candidate utility function by ten changes nothing about the preferences it represents, but gives that candidate ten times the weight in the expectation. This is the problem of interpersonal comparisons, arising within a single person. What the benefactor needs is a rule

f: probability functions over the person’s possible utility functions → rankings of the options.

We ask how far the standard solutions to interpersonal comparisons carry over to the intrapersonal case, among them normalizing every utility function to the same range and egalitarian equivalence. We also ask what changes when the benefactor acts for a group whose preferences are uncertain. There the order of aggregation matters: aggregating across people first and aggregating across possible preferences first can give different rankings.

OutputsLearning the Value of Value LearningIntrapersonal Comparisons under Preference Uncertainty

Key questions

  1. Which aggregation rules do current training pipelines assume, and how should they be compared?
  2. How should a system act for one person who is uncertain about her own preferences, when expected preference is not well defined?

Alignment

How can humans safely delegate to increasingly capable systems whose objectives may diverge from their own?

Why it mattersPerfect alignment is unlikely, so the practical question is when imperfect alignment is good enough. A safety case for deployment needs a way to say how much alignment a given role requires.

AI Alignment as a Principal–Agent Problem

Led by Daniel Herrmann and Aydin Mohseni

When should we delegate decisions to an AI system whose values may differ from ours, and how should delegation, incentives, and oversight be designed when we do? Perfect alignment is unlikely, so the question is when imperfect alignment is good enough.

More

When is imperfect alignment good enough to delegate a decision to an AI system?

Perfect alignment is unlikely, so we need to know when a system that does not fully share our values is good enough to hand a task. We call this the question of alignment sufficiency. We model the choice as a decision for a principal who is uncertain about the system’s values and abilities, and describe the system only by how it would choose.In practice we observe what a system does, not what it believes or wants. None of the results assume that the system maximizes expected utility. Delegating the problems she expects it to face pays when

reach + information > cost of misalignment.

Reach is the value of the problems the system faces that she would not, information is what its choices reveal about the world, and the cost of misalignment is what its departures from her own choices cost her.

Current work: knife-edges and margins

Dorst and colleagues propose that to defer to an expert is to prefer her opinions for any decision whatever.K. Dorst, B. A. Levinstein, B. Salow, B. E. Husic, and B. Fitelson, “Deference Done Better,” Philosophical Perspectives 35 (2021). Carried over to delegation, where the agent’s values come along with its opinions, this standard becomes a knife-edge. The ex ante standard is robust. A system that errs only on close calls, within δ of her indifference, costs at most Kδ², where K measures how often she faces close calls, while its information does not shrink with δ.

A second strand builds formal models of delegation, incentives, and misalignment between a principal and a more capable agent.

Key questions

  1. How reliable must the measurement of a trait be for a given deployment decision?
  2. What is it for an agent to allow itself to be changed, as opposed to merely being changeable?
  3. Can a system share our values while reasoning by a different decision theory?
