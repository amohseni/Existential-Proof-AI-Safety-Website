Evolutionary Theory of Advanced AI Adaptations

We study the pressures that shape the behavioral dispositions of advanced AI systems as they are trained and deployed, and how those pressures bear on alignment. Current work gives an epistemic interpretation of the power-seeking theorems.

Overview

We call the theory evolutionary because training and deployment act as selection: dispositions that do well under them persist and spread.

Claims that capable AI systems will seek power, resist shutdown, or deceive their overseers are usually stated loosely. We treat each such claim of instrumental convergenceInstrumental convergence: the thesis that agents with very different final goals will pursue similar intermediate goals, such as acquiring resources, because those goals help with almost any final goal. as a claim with six parts:

Systems of kind A, trained by processes T, on tasks and data distributed as D, converge in sense C to behavior pattern B in situations S.

The patterns B in view include coordination, collusion, deception, proxy gaming, power-seeking, resource acquisition, and self-preservation, and properties such as coherence and situational awareness. Current work concerns power-seeking.

Why it matters

Much of the case for catastrophic risk from AI rests on the claim that a wide range of goals leads a system to seek power. Nobody has yet shown this in a model with the structure the real world has. We aim to state the assumptions under which it follows, and which of them, dropped, make it fail, so that each becomes a claim about trained systems that an evaluation or an interpretability study can check.

Current work

The power-seeking theorems

Turner and colleagues proved that, for most reward functions, optimal policies tend to seek power.A. M. Turner, L. Smith, R. Shah, A. Critch, and P. Tadepalli, “Optimal Policies Tend to Seek Power,” NeurIPS 2021; A. M. Turner and P. Tadepalli, “Parametrically Retargetable Decision-Makers Tend to Seek Power,” NeurIPS 2022. The proofs count reward functions by a symmetry argument that many readers find hard to interpret. We reconstruct the prior that rationalizes this counting approach, bringing the analysis into the Bayesian fold. The probabilities then admit two interpretations.
