Measuring Beliefs and Desires in AI Systems

How can we tell what an AI system believes and wants? We develop criteria for attributing beliefs, desires, and meanings to AI systems, and test them on small transformers and on language models.

Overview

How can we tell what an AI system believes and wants from facts about its computation?

Interpretability methods read beliefs and goals off a model’s internal states. We give criteria for when such methods succeed. We take up the problem of radical interpretation,Radical interpretation: the problem, posed by Davidson and Lewis, of working out what an agent believes, wants, and means from facts that do not presuppose any of these. with the facts about the agent given by its computation:

Find the beliefs B, desires D, and meanings M that together best explain the computation C of system S.

The three are solved for together. Belief, desire, and the structure of the propositions they concern constrain one another, so a method that holds one fixed while measuring another inherits its distortions.

Why it matters

Many safety concerns are claims about what a system believes and wants. A deceptive system believes one thing and says another, and a misaligned one wants something other than what we want. Checking such claims requires knowing when a reading of a model’s internal states is a reading of its beliefs and desires. Working out whether a system breaks down into belief and desire, and what its desires are, is most of the work of telling whether it is aligned, and whether its alignment is good enough for a given use.

Current work

Criteria

Probes for belief in language models fail to generalize in basic ways.B. A. Levinstein and D. A. Herrmann, “Still No Lie Detector for Language Models: Probing Empirical and Conceptual Roadblocks,” Philosophical Studies. We argue that a representation must meet four conditions to count as belief-like.D. A. Herrmann and B. A. Levinstein, “Standards for Belief Representations in LLMs,” Minds and Machines. The book Radical AI Interpretability ties criteria of success, on representationalist and interpretationist views alike, to tests that current methods can run. The next step is to give deception and situational awareness the same treatment.

Model organisms

In a synthetic decision task, the true posterior over hidden states and the value of each option can be computed exactly, so attributions can be checked against ground truth. A small transformer trained on choices alone develops separable linear representations of both, and steering the posterior changes choice through the subspace that encodes the options’ values, as a Jeffrey–Bolker reading predicts. A pretrained language model shows the same organization. Jeremy Saal leads this work, co-mentored by Daniel Herrmann and Paul Riechers (Simplex). A paper is under review.

Language models

When a language model’s choices are read as expected-utility maximization, how coherent are the credences and utilities this attributes, and how robust is the attribution? We test whether the attribution predicts choices in and out of sample, and whether steering the putative credence and utility representations shifts choices as expected-utility theory predicts. We also check it against probes and under rephrasings that preserve meaning.
