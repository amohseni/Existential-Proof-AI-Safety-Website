Evolutionary Theory of Advanced AI Adaptations

Aydin Mohseni, Daniel Herrmann, and Sven Neth

A general theory of the pressures that shape the behavioral dispositions of advanced AI systems as they are trained and deployed, and of how those pressures bear on alignment. Current work gives an epistemic interpretation of the power-seeking theorems.

More

Which pressures shape the behavioral dispositions of AI systems as they are trained and deployed, and how do those pressures bear on alignment?

Claims that capable AI systems will seek power, resist shutdown, or deceive their overseers are usually stated loosely. We treat each such claim of instrumental convergenceInstrumental convergence: the thesis that agents with very different final goals will pursue similar intermediate goals, such as acquiring resources, because those goals help with almost any final goal. as a claim with six parts:

Systems of kind A, trained by processes T, on tasks and data distributed as D, converge in sense C to behavior pattern B in situations S.

The behavior patterns B in view include coherence, coordination, collusion, deception, learning, situational awareness, proxy gaming, power-seeking, resource acquisition, and self-preservation. Current work concerns power-seeking.

Current work: the power-seeking theorems

Turner and colleagues proved that, for most reward functions, optimal policies tend to seek power.A. M. Turner, L. Smith, R. Shah, A. Critch, and P. Tadepalli, “Optimal Policies Tend to Seek Power,” NeurIPS 2021; A. M. Turner and P. Tadepalli, “Parametrically Retargetable Decision-Makers Tend to Seek Power,” NeurIPS 2022. The proofs count reward functions by a symmetry argument that many readers find hard to interpret. We construct the prior that the counting assumes, which turns each result into a statement about probability. The probabilities then admit two interpretations.

The Robustness of Agential Representations

SPAR, fall 2026 · Aydin Mohseni with Jens Jäger and Haotian Zong

When a language model’s choices are interpreted as expected-utility maximization, how coherent are the credences and utilities attributed to it, and how robust is that attribution?

More

The project tests the attribution in four ways: whether it predicts the model’s choices in and out of sample; whether steering the putative credence and utility representations shifts choices as expected-utility theory predicts; whether it agrees with credences and utilities decoded by probes; and whether it is stable under rephrasings that preserve meaning.

Radical AI Interpretability

Daniel A. Herrmann and Benjamin A. Levinstein

How can we tell what an AI system believes and wants from facts about its computation? The book joins philosophical radical interpretation with mechanistic interpretability.

More

The book joins radical interpretationRadical interpretation: the project, from Davidson and Lewis, of working out what an agent believes, wants, and means from facts that do not presuppose any of these. with mechanistic interpretability. It gives criteria of success for attributing beliefs, desires, and meanings, and ties each to tests that current methods can run. Its central lesson is that attribution is holistic. Belief, desire, and propositional structure constrain one another, so a method that measures one while holding the others fixed inherits their distortions.

It builds on two papers. One showed that leading probes for belief in language models fail to generalize. The other proposed four conditions for a representation to count as belief-like: accuracy, coherence, uniformity, and use. The next step is to give the same treatment to deception and situational awareness.

AI Alignment as a Principal–Agent Problem

Daniel A. Herrmann and Benjamin A. Levinstein

When is imperfect alignment good enough to delegate a decision to an AI system? There is a margin of misalignment within which delegating pays.

More

A capable system may know more than we do and have options we lack. The same capabilities make it costly if its aims diverge from ours. We ask when a principal who is uncertain about a system’s values and abilities should hand it decisions. Letting it decide any problem whatever is a knife-edge standard: one misclassified gamble defeats it. Handing over the problems we expect it to face is robust, and the gain splits into three terms:

Gain from delegating = reach + information − cost of misalignment.

A system that errs only on close calls, within δ of our indifference, costs at most Kδ², where K measures how often we face close calls. What it knows does not shrink with δ, so there is a margin of misalignment within which delegating pays.

A Bayesian Reduction of Causation

Daniel A. Herrmann, Benjamin A. Levinstein, Aydin Mohseni, and Bruce Rushing

Does causal reasoning need anything beyond subjective probability? Treat interventions as events in the world, and claims about them reduce to conditioning.

More

Results on the causal hierarchy say that claims about interventions cannot be recovered from probabilities. We argue that these results rest on an impoverished set of variables. Treat interventions as events in the world, and claims about them reduce to conditioning. A representation theorem in the style of de Finetti shows how an agent can learn causal structure and probabilities together. For AI, this suggests that a system can reason well about interventions with no explicit causal machinery inside it, which bears on what interpretability should look for in a world model.
